What is a 360° Virtual Tour?

A plain-language guide to what a virtual tour actually is, how one gets made, and when it earns its cost.

The basics

A tour is a set of places, not a set of pictures

A 360° virtual tour is a series of spherical photographs, linked together so a viewer can move between them and look in any direction from each one. That is the whole idea. Everything else — hotspots, floor plans, branding, audio — is built on top of that foundation.

The unit that matters is the scene: one position where the camera stands and captures a complete sphere. A small restaurant might need four. A hotel usually needs one per room type plus the public areas. A retreat centre spread across several buildings can pass thirty. Because each scene carries a fixed amount of work, this is what determines both the price and how long a project takes.

The important difference from a photo gallery is not resolution or polish. It is that a tour preserves relationships. A gallery shows you a room; a tour shows you where that room sits, what is next to it, and how far it is from the pool. Almost every question that delays a booking or a viewing is a question about relationships, which is why the format converts better than the same content presented flat.

How it is made

From bracketed exposures to a finished tour

Each scene is photographed as a set of bracketed exposures — the same view captured several times at different brightnesses. Those are then blended into a single high-dynamic-range image and stitched into a sphere. When this is done by hand rather than automatically inside a consumer camera, a shaded interior and a bright window can both be correctly exposed in the same frame. That is the single hardest shot in property photography and the clearest visible difference between a professional tour and a cheap one.

After capture comes the build: colour-matching neighbouring scenes so moving between them does not jar, placing the navigation hotspots that link scenes together, and adding any information hotspots, floor plans or booking links. The result is delivered as an embeddable tour, a shareable link, or both.

A capture session can also feed more than one output. The same photographs can produce the tour on your site, a publication to Google Maps through the Street View Trusted programme, and high-resolution stills pulled from the panoramas. That is why it is usually worth capturing more than you think you need while the camera is already on site.

Cost

What one costs, and what drives it

Projects start around $650 for a small property of up to five scenes, and most hotel, resort and real estate projects land between $1,800 and $4,000. The number of scenes drives the price more than anything else; after that come drone aerials, Google Street View publication, 360° video, custom hotspot and branding work, and travel.

The trap to avoid is comparing headline numbers rather than deliverables. A much cheaper quote often turns out to cover raw 360° images without a built tour, auto-stitched at lower resolution, with revisions billed hourly and hosting charged separately from year two. When it is worth it

The cases where a tour actually pays

A virtual tour earns its cost most clearly when the buyer is remote, the commitment is large, or the space is the product. In Costa Rica that covers most of the tourism and property economy: a guest booking four nights from another continent, a buyer deciding whether a property justifies a flight, a retreat guest committing to a week.

It also earns its cost quietly, by answering questions nobody has to email about. How the property connects, how far the beach is, what the room actually looks like. For a business fielding the same three questions every week, that alone can justify the spend.

Where it earns less: businesses whose customers are already local and already know the space, and premises where the interior genuinely is not part of the decision. Being honest about that is more useful than pretending every business needs one.

Common questions

What is the difference between a 360° tour and a video?

A video plays on a fixed path that someone else chose. A tour lets the viewer decide where to go and where to look. Video is better for storytelling and atmosphere; a tour is better for answering questions and for anyone comparing options.

How long does a tour take to produce?

Most properties are two to four hours on site, with a delivery date agreed before the shoot. Larger multi-building properties are scheduled with a timeline to match.

Do visitors need an app or a headset?

No. Tours run in any modern browser on phone, tablet or desktop. A VR headset is optional and gives a more immersive version of the same tour.

Can a tour be published to Google?

Yes, through the Street View Trusted programme, which places the imagery inside Google Maps and your Google Business Profile rather than only on your own website.

How many scenes does my property need?

Count the distinct spaces a visitor would want to see, not the rooms. One per room type plus the public areas is a reasonable starting estimate for hospitality.
